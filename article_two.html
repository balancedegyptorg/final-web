What Ancient Egypt Can Teach Us About Food Waste

Written by Mohamed Naguib, Loren Emad Morgan & Youssef Labib • Published Jan 24, 2025 • 10 Min Read

On This Page

Food and Culture Preservation and Byproducts Offerings and Symbolism Sources

Long before sustainability had a name, ancient Egyptians were already practicing it. An Egyptian sage named Kagemni once taught that a person sitting down to eat should show restraint, even when the food in front of them is tempting, because a single cup of water can quench a whole field of crops.[1]

That idea, that resources deserve respect and food deserves moderation, shaped nearly every part of how ancient Egyptians grew, stored, cooked, and shared what they ate. This is a look at three parts of that relationship: the everyday culture around food, the preservation science that kept it from spoiling, and the religious beliefs that shaped how it was offered and shared.

Food and Culture

Ancient Egyptian homes were often built with the kitchen at the back of the house, positioned to catch ventilation and keep cooking smells from spreading indoors, with a plastered hollow used to keep food and drinks cool.[2] It's a small detail, but it says something bigger: food storage was already part of how people designed their daily lives.

What people ate depended heavily on social class. Meat, fish, and birds were largely reserved for pharaohs, nobles, and the wealthy, while for most people, meat was an occasional, seasonal food.[2] The staple diet for ordinary Egyptians was built around bread, vegetables, chickpeas, and lentils, with emmer wheat as the most widely available crop. Fruit was dried for year round eating, and because vegetables couldn't be preserved the same way, legumes filled that gap instead.[3]

That heavy reliance on plants over meat wasn't just about availability. It also happened to be far lighter on the environment. Some modern research estimates that a meat based diet can have as much as 14 times the environmental impact of a plant based one.[4] Ancient Egyptians landed on a more sustainable way of eating well before anyone had a word for sustainability.

Preservation and Byproducts

Ancient Egyptians relied on a handful of preservation methods that still hold up today. Fruit was dried in the sun, while fish and meat were dried using the wind. Salting was used mainly for fish and meat, since it slows bacterial growth and extends shelf life for long periods. Honey, thanks to its high sugar content, was used both as a sweetener and as a natural preservative.[5]

Grain storage was treated as a serious institutional responsibility. Granaries were often managed by temples and administrative centers, and depictions of them appear throughout Egyptian art and record keeping.[6] One particularly clever method was anaerobic storage: grain was sealed away from air, so it consumed the oxygen inside its container and released carbon dioxide, which halted decay and kept pests and rodents out.[6]

Balance, or Ma'at, wasn't just a spiritual idea to ancient Egyptians. It shaped how they grew, stored, and shared their food.

Almost nothing went to waste. Animal bones became tools like fishhooks and needles, and tanned hides became leather clothing and footwear. The papyrus plant, best known for producing an early form of paper, also had its roots eaten as food, while its stems were used to build boats and mats.[7] Leftovers from brewing beer and baking bread were fed to animals, and fat left over from cooking meat was often saved and reused rather than thrown away. Byproducts weren't an afterthought, they were simply part of how food was used all the way through.

Offerings and Symbolism

Food in ancient Egypt carried meaning well beyond nutrition. Religious and cultural beliefs shaped how people ate just as much as taste or availability did.[8] Egyptians believed in an afterlife that continued much like earthly existence, and food was placed in tombs as part of that belief, a sign that food was valued as something spiritual, not only something consumed.

Maintaining Ma'at, the concept of divine balance and order, also shaped food practices. Pharaohs were believed to uphold Ma'at in part by offering specific foods to please the gods, a practice rooted in respect for natural resources rather than excess.[9] Importantly, offered food wasn't simply left to spoil. After a ritual, it was typically returned to the priests as compensation for their service, or sold in the marketplace, an intentional system that kept even ceremonial food in circulation rather than letting it go to waste.[10]

What stands out across all of this isn't just clever storage technique, it's intention. Ancient Egyptians treated food as something worth protecting, at every stage, for practical, economic, and spiritual reasons all at once. That's the same instinct behind everything Balanced Egypt does today, just with modern tools.
