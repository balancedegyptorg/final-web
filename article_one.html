Where Does Our Food Go to Waste? Tracing Loss From Farm to Table

Written by Mariam Hossam & Amr Muhammed • Published Dec 23, 2024 • 6 Min Read

On This Page

Production Processing Shipping and Handling Restaurants and Retail Households Sources

Nearly a third of everything we grow, catch, or produce as food never gets eaten. The Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that 14 percent of the world's food is lost after harvest, before it even reaches a market, while another 17 percent is wasted later, by retailers and consumers.[1] All of that loss adds up. Decomposing food is responsible for 8 to 10 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions, which means every wasted meal carries a climate cost too.[1]

To understand how to fix this, it helps to follow food from farm to plate. Waste does not happen at one single point. It builds up across several stages: production, processing, shipping and handling, and two very different forms of consumption, in restaurants and retail, and inside our own homes. Here is what happens at each one, and where the biggest opportunities to fix it are.

Production

About 30 percent of all food grown worldwide, roughly 2.5 billion tons a year, is lost or wasted, and a large share of that happens before the harvest is even finished.[2] Overproduction is one of the biggest drivers, farmers growing more than the market ends up needing. Pests, disease, and unpredictable weather account for more, along with inefficient harvesting methods that leave crops behind in the field.

A less obvious cause is cosmetic. Produce that does not meet size, shape, or color standards often gets discarded, even when it is perfectly edible. Every one of those rejected vegetables also represents wasted water, energy, and labor that went into growing it in the first place.

Processing

Roughly 38 percent of all food waste happens during processing, the stage where raw ingredients are turned into something ready for the shelf.[3] Outdated equipment and poor storage practices are common culprits, but processing also generates waste almost by design: plant byproducts like peels, seeds, and bran, animal byproducts like bone and tissue, and large volumes of wastewater.

None of that has to end up in a landfill. Better technology, tighter quality control, and smarter operations can cut losses significantly, and byproducts that used to be thrown away can often be repurposed into animal feed, compost, or new products entirely.

Shipping and Handling

Getting food from a processing plant to a supermarket shelf sounds simple, but this stage accounts for 7 to 15 percent of all food loss worldwide.[4] Four things tend to go wrong: food gets mishandled during loading, cooling systems fail during transit, deliveries are delayed by traffic or poor scheduling, and sometimes, simply, more food arrives than a retailer can sell before it spoils.

Every rejected vegetable carries the water, energy, and labor spent growing it, wasted before it ever reaches a plate.

Every one of these problems is fixable with better infrastructure. Reliable cold storage, smarter routing, and closer coordination between suppliers and buyers can turn shipping from one of the weakest links in the food system into one of the strongest.

Restaurants and Retail

Most of us encounter food waste at the point of a fork or a shopping cart. Globally, food service is responsible for about 26 percent of total food waste, and retail adds another 13 percent.[5] In restaurants, oversized portions, overproduction, and eating out often all add up.[6] In grocery stores, strict appearance standards and sell by dates send edible food to the bin before it should ever get there.[7]

The fixes here are some of the most immediate available. Right sized portions, better inventory management, and food rescue programs that redirect surplus to people who need it can all make a measurable difference, fast.

Households

Households are the single largest source of food waste in the entire system. An estimated 61 percent of all food waste, around 570 million tonnes a year, happens in our own kitchens.[5] In the European Union, households alone generate more than half of the region's total food waste.[8]

Most household waste comes down to three moments: shopping, storing, and serving. Buying more than we need, storing food incorrectly, and misjudging portions at mealtime all lead to spoilage that was entirely avoidable.[9] The good news is that the fix is simple and free. A bit of meal planning and smarter storage habits go a long way.

Food waste is not one problem, it is five smaller ones stacked on top of each other, from the farm to the fridge. That is exactly why Balanced Egypt works across the entire chain instead of focusing on just one stage.
