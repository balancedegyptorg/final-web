From Fasting to Feasting: The Crisis Behind Ramadan Feast

Written by Mariam Kamal & Loren Emad Morgan • Published Mar 3, 2026 • 4 Min Read

On This Page

The Scale of the Problem How the Waste Happens Why It Keeps Happening Beyond Ramadan Sources

Across the Arab region, food waste reaches close to 40 million tons a year, and Egypt, as the region's most populous country, contributes nearly 9 million tons of that total on its own.[1] In 2022 alone, restaurants and companies in Egypt discarded 87.8 tons of edible food, enough to feed over 219,000 people, while hotels threw out another 2.5 tons, enough for close to 6,500 more.[2] Normally, somewhere between 20 and 25 percent of prepared food goes uneaten.[1] During Ramadan, that number climbs by as much as 50 percent.[3]

There's a real irony here. Ramadan is a month built around moderation, self discipline, and empathy for people experiencing food insecurity. And yet it has become one of the highest periods of food waste in the country all year, with almost 45 percent of household income going toward food during a month centered on fasting.[1] To understand why, we surveyed 97 people across Egypt, from a range of income levels and backgrounds, to find out what's actually driving the waste.

The Scale of the Problem

Restaurants and food service businesses are the single largest contributor to Ramadan food waste, followed closely by households.[1] What our survey found is that this isn't really about scarcity or poor infrastructure. It's about behavior, happening at scale, in millions of homes and kitchens at once.

How the Waste Happens

It starts with shopping. In our survey, 58.8 percent of people said they buy more food during Ramadan than they normally would. Interestingly, 45.4 percent said they plan their iftar meals and shop with a list, but that planning doesn't reliably prevent overbuying. Half of respondents, 50.5 percent, admitted to cooking extra food "as a precaution," driven by a fear of running short after a long day of fasting.

That precautionary mindset carries through to the table. Demand for meat rises by roughly 50 percent during Ramadan, adding pressure across the supply chain.[4] Larger portions, buffet style serving, and genuine hunger after fasting all push people toward over serving. Most individual households only end up with small leftovers, 56.7 percent said theirs were minor, but multiplied across millions of homes, those small surpluses add up to a serious national total.

Ramadan is defined by moderation, and yet it has become one of Egypt's biggest food waste events of the year.

Why It Keeps Happening

Over half our respondents, 51.5 percent, pointed to excessive cooking as the main driver of Ramadan waste. Much of that comes down to culture. Hospitality and generosity are deeply tied to social standing in Egypt, and preparing an abundance of food, sometimes multiple full dishes for a single meal, is often read as a sign of respect rather than excess.[4] Seasonal promotions and lower prices ahead of Ramadan encourage bulk buying, and because many households try to avoid multiple grocery trips while fasting, that bulk buying frequently turns into overstocking, and eventually, spoilage.[4]

There's also a structural gap around donations. Surplus food doesn't always reach the people who need it, due to late distribution, logistical gaps, and irregular schedules during Ramadan nights.[4] The attitudes are there, 80.4 percent of people we surveyed support surplus redistribution initiatives, but only 45.4 percent believe those initiatives would actually help them personally reduce their own waste. That's a meaningful gap between good intentions and practical systems people can actually use.

Reuse habits exist, but they aren't enough to offset how much gets made in the first place. Many families avoid repeating the same dish for suhoor or the next day's iftar, which limits how much gets reused.[4] Still, 61.9 percent of our respondents said they do reuse leftovers, a pattern consistent with wider research showing about 61 percent of Egyptians repurpose surplus food in some way.[3] The willingness to reduce waste is clearly there. It just isn't translating into enough change on its own.

Beyond Ramadan

Ramadan doesn't create Egypt's food waste problem, it intensifies one that already exists year round. About 60 percent of the country's food waste happens during occasions and holidays generally, with fruits and vegetables making up the largest share.[1] Globally, 14 percent of all food produced is lost after harvest, before it even reaches a market, and in Egypt specifically, an estimated 45 to 55 percent of annual agricultural production is lost somewhere across the supply chain.[2] Ramadan simply adds a seasonal spike on top of inefficiencies that were already there.

What our survey makes clear is that this isn't a problem of people not knowing better. Most respondents recognize that waste rises during Ramadan, and 73.2 percent said they'd be willing to take part in a "No Food Waste Challenge." The awareness is there. What's missing is a consistent bridge between good intentions and daily habits, and that gap, between what people believe and what they actually do, is exactly where Balanced Egypt focuses its work.
